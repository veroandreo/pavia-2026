Vector-borne and zoonotic diseases are responsible for one-sixth of disease and disability worldwide. This is one of the reasons why good health and well-being is among the 17 UN goals for sustainable development. More specifically, the target is to end epidemics of NTDs among other communicable diseases.

Neglected Tropical Diseases (NTD)


Which are these NTDs and why might remote sensing be relevant for their study? Most of them are transmitted by animals — they need a vector to carry the pathogen from host to humans or among humans. Animals, especially ectothermic ones, are highly affected by environmental conditions… think of Aedes aegypti mosquitoes… and what information can be most easily extracted from RS? Environmental information.

Environmental health: focuses on environmental hazards, environmental risk assessment, and the physical and psycho-social health impacts of environmental contamination.

Disease ecology: study of infectious diseases (including NTDs) and the spatial distribution of environmental, social, political & economic conditions associated with disease.

Health care delivery and access: spatial patterns of health care provision and patient behavior.

This was the beginning of what was called health geography — the application of geographical information, perspectives, and methods to the study of health, disease, and health care. Within this discipline we can identify 3 fields of study: Environmental health, Health access and Disease ecology.

This relationship with environment is usually represented by a triad — the epidemiologic triad. For disease to occur (in humans) we need proper environmental conditions, a pathogen, and competent hosts and vectors. Environment encompasses climate, LULCC, habitat fragmentation, human behaviour, and all the relationships among them. This is what we usually try to observe with RS.

What is Remote Sensing?

The acquisition of information about objects or phenomena without being in direct contact with them, through detection of the electromagnetic energy they emit or reflect.

Remote sensing is the science and art of obtaining information about an object, area, or phenomenon through the analysis of data acquired by a device not in physical contact with the object. Satellites, aircraft, or drones carry sensors that record energy reflected or emitted by the Earth’s surface. A remote sensing system has four fundamental components: an energy source (usually the sun, although SAR systems generate their own), the interaction of that energy with the surface (each cover type reflects or emits differently), the sensor that records that energy, and the entire process of data transmission and processing until ready-to-use products are obtained.

The electromagnetic spectrum


The electromagnetic spectrum spans from gamma rays to radio waves. Earth observation satellites work mainly in four regions: the visible (what we see with our eyes), the near-infrared (fundamental for studying vegetation), the thermal infrared (for measuring surface temperature), and microwaves (SAR radars, which can penetrate clouds). Each region has atmospheric transmission windows where the atmosphere is transparent enough to allow measurements.

What does the sensor measure?

Sensors measure the electromagnetic energy reaching the detector in each band:


Radiance (W·sr⁻¹·m⁻²) Energy received by the sensor — includes atmospheric effects


Reflectance (dimensionless, 0–1) Fraction of sunlight reflected by the surface — obtained after applying atmospheric correction


Spectral signatures

The “fingerprint” of each land cover type

Each type of land cover has a characteristic reflectance curve as a function of wavelength. Healthy vegetation reflects very little in the red (chlorophyll absorbs that light for photosynthesis) but a lot in the near-infrared (the cellular structure of leaves reflects it). This marked difference is the basis of NDVI. Water absorbs almost all energy in the infrared, which is why it appears dark in those bands. Bare soil has an increasing and more uniform reflectance. Stressed vegetation (dry, diseased) loses that NIR jump, allowing us to detect problems before they are visible to the naked eye.

RS-derived variables


Spectral indices Band ratio combinations

(NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red)

  • NDVI → vegetation
  • NDWI → water / moisture
  • NDBI → built-up area

Physical variables Real magnitudes derived with models

  • LST → land surface temperature (°C/K)
  • Albedo → total reflected fraction
  • LAI → leaf area index
  • Precipitation (microwave)

Thematic products Classifications and interpretations

  • LULC → land use / land cover
  • Vegetation maps
  • Change detection (LULCC)
  • Risk maps (with GIS)

This distinction is important: spectral indices are direct operations on reflectance bands, dimensionless. Physical variables require more complex models and have real units. Thematic products result from classifying or interpreting images, and often combine multiple information sources.

What is a GIS?


A Geographic Information System (GIS) is a system that allows capturing, storing, analysing and visualising data with a spatial component:


RS → how we obtain the data

  • Satellite images
  • Environmental variables
  • Temperature, vegetation, rainfall…

GIS → how we integrate and analyse them

  • Combine information layers
  • Disease case data + environmental variables
  • Generate risk maps

GIS is the “spatial laboratory” where we integrate all information. It can be thought of as a very powerful version of a digital map where we can overlay very different information layers: satellite images, disease case data, administrative boundaries, road networks, etc., and analyse them together to find patterns or model risks.

Challenges and gaps — RS

  • Trade-off between different RS resolutions, the problem under study, the data and methods available
  • Gaps in optical RS: clouds, shadows (spatial and temporal interpolations)
  • Need for corrections if high-level data is not suitable
  • Limited access to VHR, LiDAR, hyperspectral (US$, tricky to scale yet)
  • Investment and capacity building: huge volumes of data vs. limited bandwidth, storage and computational capacity (cloud computing, parallelisation | resources)

Field data will always be needed! :)

Challenges and gaps — Ecology and Health


  • Missing baseline distribution information of hosts, vectors, infection
  • Updating and digitisation of disease cases and intervention data, data still missing in large parts of the world
  • Harmonisation of records at different administrative levels
  • Facilitating access to (aggregated) health data
  • Political decision and resource allocation
